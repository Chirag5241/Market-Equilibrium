Decentralized Equilibrium Discovery After Shocks

Chirag RastogiSejun Park

Equilibrium models say where a market should end up. They do not say how a decentralized market with private preferences gets there. We study that adjustment in a peer-to-peer market with no central exchange and no public price. A shock changes some agents' needs, the old allocation stops being stable, and the agents have to find a new one through bilateral trades that both sides accept.

Central question

How do communication and preference inference affect the speed of reaching Pareto efficiency after a shock, and the distribution of gains from trade?

01Design

Who computes the trade, and what they know

We cross two choices. The trade is computed either by a solver or through LLM dialogue, and agents either see both utility functions or only their own. Holdings are public in every cell; only utility functions can be private. In all four cells a trade executes only if it is feasible and strictly improves both agents' true utilities. Acceptance is myopic for now; strategic concealment and rejection are extensions.

Target and metric

Bilateral Nash bargaining is path dependent, so we do not assume a unique global target allocation. Instead, at each market state x(t) we compute the full-information Nash-bargaining trade Δ*ij(x(t)) for every pair i ≠ j, set to zero when no mutually beneficial trade exists.

The pairwise residual error is the average amount of reallocation that would still happen if every pair bargained optimally from the current state. It reaches zero exactly when the allocation is pairwise stable. That does not imply global Pareto optimality, since profitable trading cycles among three or more agents can remain (Feldman 1973).

Convergence time
Tε=min{t:Epair(t)≤ε}

Each round, agents are paired by a uniformly random perfect matching, so any pair can meet.

02Market design · Agent preferences

Instruments that differ by duration, agents that differ by habitat

N agents hold portfolios xi ∈ ℝK≥0 of K divisible instruments. There is no exchange, order book or common price, and total supply of each instrument is fixed. Beliefs about returns are common, so agents trade to share risk, not on private information.

Instrument k has a duration dk, its exposure along the maturity and interest-rate-sensitivity dimension, and an idiosyncratic payoff variance σ2k, a separate risk such as issuer default. Idiosyncratic risks are independent across instruments, so portfolio risk depends on diversification rather than on a full covariance model.

Agent i has a preferred duration d*i, a tolerance τi and a risk aversion λi. It values instruments near its preferred duration most, and value falls off smoothly with distance. The quadratic risk term creates the diversification incentive: without it an agent would pile into whichever instrument it values most.

The simulator draws agents from four clienteles: banks at the short end, bond funds and insurers in the middle, pension funds at the long end. For the first experiments every instrument shares one variance σ2, so instruments differ only in duration.

03Bilateral Nash bargaining · Optimal solver

One meeting, solved exactly in O(K log 1/ε)

When i and j meet, a trade is a transfer vector Δ ∈ ℝK: i receives Δ and j gives it up. Feasibility requires xi + Δ ≥ 0 and xj − Δ ≥ 0. Under full information, Nash bargaining picks the trade that maximizes the product of the two gains.

Nash bargain
Δij*(x)∈arg⁡maxΔgi(Δ)gj(Δ)s.t. gi,gj≥0

Weighted problem, closed form per instrument
Δk(r)=clip(aik−rajk2σk2(λi+rλj),−xik,xjk)
Bisection target
F(r)=gi(Δ(r))−rgj(Δ(r))=0

As r grows, gi cannot rise and gj cannot fall. Δ = 0 is feasible, so the weighted objective is nonnegative and F changes sign exactly once, at the positive-gain solution. Each evaluation costs O(K). If no strictly mutually beneficial trade exists, the solver returns Δ* = 0.

04Theory · Limited-information Nash bargaining

Bargaining when the counterpart's utility is private

In cells B and D the proposer knows its own type and everyone's holdings, but not the counterpart's type θj = (vj, λj). It proposes the Nash bargain it would compute if its belief θ̂j were true, and the counterpart accepts only if its true gain is positive. This section states what that setup implies, and which parts we have checked numerically.

Setup (cell B). A meeting allows up to m proposals. The proposer runs the solver from section 03 with θ̂j in place of θj, proposes the result Δ̂, and the counterpart answers yes or no. Acceptance is myopic and truthful: j accepts exactly when gj(Δ̂; θj) > 0. Every answer updates the belief. Cell D replaces the yes/no updates with a noisy disclosure in dialogue, but the acceptance rule is the same.

What an answer reveals. The counterpart's gain is linear in its type. So each yes or no tells the proposer which side of a hyperplane θj lies on. The types that agree with every answer so far form a convex cone, the version space. θj is fixed after the shock, so a constraint learned at one state stays valid at every later state, even though xj keeps changing. The version space can only shrink.

Scale is never learned, and it doesn't need to be. Multiplying θj by c > 0 multiplies gj by c and flips no answer, so yes/no data can identify θj only up to scale. The Nash bargain is unchanged by that rescaling: argmax gi·cgj = argmax gigj. The answers therefore carry exactly the information the bargain needs: a direction in ℝK+1. A counting argument gives a lower bound. There are about (1/δ)K directions distinguishable at angular resolution δ, and each answer is one bit, so any proposer needs about K log2(1/δ) answers in the worst case. Goal 2 asks for a matching upper bound when proposals are restricted to predicted Nash trades rather than arbitrary cuts.

Five results

Proposition 1 · Safety

Private information can delay trade but never hurt anyone

Every executed trade strictly raises both participants' true utilities and leaves everyone else unchanged. So in every cell, Ui(xi(t)) is nondecreasing for every agent. Total welfare ΔW(t) never falls, and no agent ever drops below its post-shock utility Ui(xoldi). Epair has no such guarantee, because one trade can create new opportunities for other pairs.

Check: the simulator's Welfare metric never decreases for any regime.

Proposition 2 · Revelation

Each answer is a halfspace; only direction matters

Accept means ⟨φj(Δ̂), θj⟩ > 0, and reject means ≤ 0. The answers identify θj up to positive scale, and the Nash bargain needs nothing more. An LLM that exaggerates how much it wants everything, by a common factor, therefore changes nothing. Only distortions of relative values or of curvature λj move the bargain.

Regime B already projects its belief onto each halfspace. See Belief error.

Proposition 3 · Acceptance margin

A fixed belief error eventually blocks every trade

The proposal is accepted whenever the belief error is below γ(Δ̂), the predicted counterpart gain per unit of exposure ‖φj‖. Near stability, predicted gains shrink, so γ shrinks too. A belief error that does not shrink as fast blocks the remaining trades. B then stalls at a residual surplus set by its belief error, rather than converging. A robust proposer could instead maximize gi·(ĝj − ρ‖φj‖). Every such proposal is accepted when ‖e‖ ≤ ρ, at the cost of some surplus. That objective is not separable across instruments, so it needs a generic solver.

Proposition 4 · Efficiency vs. distribution

Small errors mostly redistribute; near stability they block

Assume Δ* is a unique, nondegenerate maximizer and the set of binding holding constraints does not change under small perturbation. By the implicit function theorem, Δ̂ − Δ* = O(‖e‖). Δ* maximizes the Nash product on its active face and the displacement stays on that face, so the first-order term vanishes and the loss is O(‖e‖2). The proposer's share of the surplus moves at first order. The local regime needs ‖e‖ to be small relative to the pair's own surplus. Near stability most pairs have little surplus, so the average loss across pairs grows far more slowly than ‖e‖2: linearly or slower, because even tiny errors already block or spoil the smallest trades.

Check: the panel below measures both slopes on the current market.

Proposition 5 · Absorbing states

B stops wherever A stops, and in more places

At a pairwise-stable state every B proposal is rejected and nothing moves, so A's stopping states are absorbing for B. The converse fails in two ways. If the belief predicts no improving trade, B makes no proposal and learns nothing. If every proposal falls outside the margin of Proposition 3, B keeps getting rejected. So B's Tε can be infinite.

Offline diagnostic (30 runs, default settings, round 120): of ordered pairs that still had an improving trade, B's proposal would be rejected for 89% and never made for 2%. B's stall is driven by rejections, not by silence.

Cell D and strategic disclosure

Propositions 1–3 carry over to cell D: the acceptance rule is enforced on true utilities, and the LLM's belief comes from what the counterpart says rather than from yes/no answers. The difference is that talk can be strategic. Myerson and Satterthwaite show that with private valuations, no bilateral mechanism is incentive compatible, individually rational, budget balanced and ex post efficient all at once. Our current agents disclose truthfully up to noise and accept myopically, so the theorem does not bind yet.

Once agents are asked to maximize their own gain, it will. Crawford and Varian show that in Nash bargaining an agent gains by reporting a less concave utility. Here that means understating λj. Proposition 2 says what to look for: lies about overall intensity are harmless, while lies about curvature and relative values move the split. Harsanyi and Selten's generalized Nash solution is the natural full-information-free benchmark for that strategic case.

Experimental protocol

Controls. Each cell starts from the same xold, shock, matching sequence and proposer coin flips (all seeded by round), with the same proposal budget m.

Treatments. Prior noise (B), proposals per meeting, disclosure noise (D), and surplus ask (C, D). Proposed additions: a shared ledger that pools every answer about j across proposers; an exploration probe, a small trade sent when the belief predicts no trade; and the robust margin ρ.

Outcomes. Tε, the Epair plateau, surplus left, rejection rate, messages and belief error, plus the welfare and fairness metrics of section 05. Tε is right-censored when a run never reaches ε, so we report the fraction reaching ε and Kaplan–Meier curves, not means.

Statistics. Comparisons are paired by seed across cells, with at least 20 seeds per configuration. We report medians and interquartile ranges, and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on paired differences.

05Evaluation · Welfare and fairness

What to measure besides the pairwise residual

Epair measures stability: whether any pair still has a trade both sides want. A state where no pair can gain is not necessarily efficient or fair. One agent owning everything is pairwise stable. We therefore track welfare and fairness alongside it, each computed every round under the post-shock preferences. All of these appear in the simulator's fairness scorecard and metric switcher.

Why EF1 and PROP1 need adapting. Both were defined for indivisible goods, by Lipton et al. and Budish for EF1 and by Conitzer, Freeman and Shah for PROP1. Our instruments are divisible, so exact EF and PROP are already well defined. The "up to one item" versions become meaningful once trades happen in lots. We take one lot to be Sk/(3N), a third of an equal share. As the lot shrinks, EF1 tends to EF.

Utility is also not monotone: past i's ideal holding, more of an instrument lowers utility, and there is no free disposal. A lot can therefore be a good or a chore. We use the goods-and-chores versions of Aziz et al., in which the lot may be removed from either bundle.

PROP is easy here, EF is not. In every allocation we have generated, including xold, every agent beats the equal split. The equal split forces each agent to hold instruments far from its preferred duration, which it cannot dispose of, so sorting by habitat beats it easily. Envy is the measure that varies: some agents prefer another clientele's portfolio. Bilateral trades from unequal endowments preserve much of that envy. The natural benchmark is the competitive equilibrium from equal incomes (CEEI), which for divisible goods and concave utilities is both envy-free and Pareto optimal (Varian 1974).

Stability is not welfare. Offline, over 20 seeds per shock, B ended with a higher ΔW than A in 15 of 20 runs for the insurer shock and 13 of 20 for the bank shock, though only 6 of 20 for idiosyncratic shocks, while never becoming pairwise stable. Which pairwise-stable state the market reaches is path dependent, and different states can rank differently on every metric above.

08Application

An endogenous term structure

The term structure says how yields vary with maturity. We add time, money and K zero-coupon bonds and ask how the yield curve emerges, and how it adjusts after a financial shock, when bonds only change hands bilaterally and no one posts a price.

Each bond promises $1 at maturity Tk, but default makes the payoff uncertain, with mean μk and variance σ2k. Money is the numeraire with unit marginal utility. Trades move money and any bundle of bonds, (mi, xi) ← (mi + Δm, xi + Δ), subject to feasibility and mutual acceptance.

Agents keep their duration preference and risk aversion and discount with either the Treasury benchmark, an exogenous curve that needs no corporate bond prices, or individual rates ρi. With money, utility is transferable, so the Nash bargain maximizes the pair's joint surplus bond by bond and splits it equally through Δm.

Each agent's marginal valuation in money is a private price. At an interior pairwise equilibrium these agree across holders, which yields an implicit price P*k and an equilibrium yield. During adjustment they disagree, and their dispersion measures incomplete price discovery.

09Plan

Concrete goals

Goal 1

Full-information baseline

For a tractable class, such as mean-variance utilities with a riskless asset, characterize when bilateral Nash-bargaining dynamics reach Pareto optimality. Bound Tε under random matching, possibly with gossip-style spectral arguments.

On this site: regime A in the simulator, and the money-based market in the term structure application, where joint-surplus bargaining behaves like randomized gossip.

Goal 2

Inference

Each accepted or rejected proposal reveals a halfspace constraint on the counterpart's θ. Bound the number of proposals needed to find an improving trade. This is the baseline LLM inference must beat.

On this site: regime B projects its belief onto each halfspace (Belief error). Section 04 shows that answers identify θ only up to scale, which is all Nash bargaining needs, and gives a K log(1/δ) counting lower bound.

Goal 3

Experiments

Compare cells A–D, with and without a money-like asset, measuring E(t), R(t), Tε, communication cost and inference accuracy, plus welfare and fairness: ΔW, normalized utility, EF/EF1, PROP/PROP1 and per-meeting Nash efficiency. LLM results are used to form conjectures.

On this site: the scorecard and batch runs report Tε, residual surplus, messages and rejection rates for every cell, the fairness scorecard reports the metrics of section 05, and the negotiation lab runs cells C and D with Claude.
