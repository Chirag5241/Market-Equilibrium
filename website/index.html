Decentralized Equilibrium Discovery After Shocks

Chirag RastogiSejun Park

Corporate bonds trade over the counter: two dealers or investors meet, agree on a bundle of bonds and a cash payment, and move on. There is no exchange and no posted price. We build a calibrated version of that market, hit it with a shock, and watch investors trade their way to the new allocation one bilateral Nash bargain at a time.

Research questions

Can decentralized bilateral trading reproduce or explain real-world term-structure adjustment after a financial shock?

Can LLM communication and preference inference speed convergence when each investor knows only its own preferences?

Status. The full-information results are done. Private-information bargaining, LLM negotiation and empirical validation remain open; this page includes our first theory and simulations for the private-information case.

01Design

Who computes the trade, and what each side knows

We compare four negotiation methods: a solver or LLM dialogue, with full or private information. In every method the market executes a proposed trade only if it is feasible and strictly raises both investors' true utilities, and every method is scored with the true post-shock utilities.

Private information
Solver

True-utility solver

The Nash bargain computed from both investors' true utilities. This is the baseline behind every full-information result in the deck.

Estimated-utility solver

The proposer knows its own utility but not the counterpart's, so it plugs an estimate of the counterpart's c and b into the same bargaining formula. The market executes the trade only if both true gains are positive. The deck leaves open where the estimate comes from: a belief updated from accepted and rejected offers (used on this page), or an LLM that talks with the counterpart and then reports its estimate of (d*, τ, λ). Either way, inference is separated from bargaining.

LLM dialogue

Full-information LLM negotiation

Both LLM negotiators see both investors' utilities and negotiate a bond bundle and a cash payment.

Private-information LLM negotiation

Each LLM sees only its own investor's utility and learns about the counterpart from the conversation.

What is private and what is public

Private: each investor's type θi = (d*i, τi, λi): preferred maturity, maturity tolerance and risk aversion.

Public: everyone's holdings x, and the bond data T, D, μ, σ², h, L and α.

Our choices where the deck is silent: the prior ranges of the types are public, group membership is private, and executed prices are private unless the public price signal is switched on. Section 06 shows that, with public holdings, these choices matter more than they look.

02Market · Bonds and investors

Six corporate bonds, ninety investors, and cash

Investors i = 1, …, N hold zero-coupon corporate bonds k = 1, …, K and cash. Supplies Xk = Σi xik and total cash M = Σi mi are fixed. Cash enters utility linearly with marginal utility 1, and borrowing is unrestricted, so mi can go negative.

Bonds. Bond k pays 1 at maturity Tk, or 1 − L if the issuer defaults first. Default arrives at a constant hazard h, the same for all bonds. That gives an expected payoff μk and a payoff variance σ2k. Payoffs are discounted with the Treasury discount factor Dk, which is a discount factor, not a duration and not a corporate-bond price.

Investors. Investor i prefers maturities near d*i, with tolerance τi, and has risk aversion λi. The quadratic term penalizes holding too much of one bond.

What α means. An investor values one unit of bond k at cik = Dkμk + αvik, before the risk penalty. The first term, the bond's discounted expected payoff, is the same for everyone. The second is a personal bonus for maturity. vik ∈ [0, 1] says how close bond k is to what the investor wants: 1 at d*i, falling off with width τi. α says how much that closeness is worth in money: the most extra an investor pays per unit of face value for a bond exactly at its preferred maturity. Think of a pension fund that values a 20-year bond beyond its cash flows because it hedges 20-year liabilities. That is the preferred-habitat idea of Modigliani and Sutch.

Its size. α is the same for every investor; investors differ in where their bonus sits (d*, τ), not in how big it can be. In the baseline α = 0.03, three cents per unit. That is small next to the discounted payoffs (0.38 to 0.92) but comparable to the risk premium that observed prices leave on each bond (0.007 to 0.066; see the table below), and holdings are driven by that premium. Example: an investor with d* = 6, τ = 2.75 and λ = 20 would hold 0.22 units of the 6-year bond at pre-shock prices if α were 0. With α = 0.03 it holds 0.41, almost double, while its 20-year holding (0.54) does not change, because v for a 20-year bond is essentially zero for this investor. With α = 0.3, preference dominates: the deck's "many zero holdings" variant uses α = 0.3 (with h = 0.04), and 54% of positions are zero because investors hold only bonds near their preferred maturity.

Baseline. N = 90 investors in three preferred-maturity groups of 30: short (d* ~ U(1, 4)), medium (U(4, 9)) and long (U(9, 25)). τi ~ U(1.5, 4), λi = 20ui with ui ~ U(0.5, 1.5), α = 0.03, h = 0.003, L = 0.6, starting cash 50.

Calibration. Before the shock, each investor holds the amount at which its marginal value equals the observed price P⁰k = Dke−skTk, where sk is the observed corporate spread in a calm week (March 24–28, 2025). Supplies are the sum of those holdings, so the pre-shock state is pairwise stable by construction.

03One meeting after the shock · Nash bargaining with cash

One bargain sets a bond bundle and a payment

Meetings happen after the shock. Preferences have changed, while holdings start at the pre-shock allocation x⁰. Before the shock no meeting would trade, because the calibration makes every holder's marginal value equal to the pre-shock price P⁰k. Investors i and j meet. i receives the bond vector Δ from j and pays z in cash: x′i = xi + Δ, x′j = xj − Δ, m′i = mi − z, m′j = mj + z. Feasibility requires −xi ≤ Δ ≤ xj. With linear cash, the Nash product separates into two easy problems.

No search over a gain ratio is needed: the product reduces to the sum because cash is linear and borrowing is unrestricted. With a cash constraint, or concave utility of cash, the best Δ would depend on the split and this formula would no longer apply. A meeting settles one cash amount for the whole bundle; to price each bond, z is split so that each bond's own surplus is shared equally, giving an executed price of ½(aik + ajk) − ½(bik − bjk)Δk per unit.

Where trading starts, and where the shock hits

1 · CalibrationPre-shock preferences. Every holder of bond k values it at exactly P⁰k. Holdings x⁰, cash m⁰ = 50.
2 · Shock, t = 0Preferences change. Holdings and cash do not move.
3 · Rounds 1, 2, …Random pairs meet; each meeting is one Nash bargain under the new preferences.
4 · Terminal stateAllocation x*, where every holder of bond k values it at P*k.

P⁰k, the pre-shock price. The price of bond k implied by the observed corporate spread sk in a calm week (March 24–28, 2025). It is used once, to set up the starting portfolios. After that there is no posted price in the model; prices only appear as the cash investors agree on in meetings.

x⁰ and m⁰, the starting position. Each investor holds the amount of each bond at which, under its pre-shock preferences, one more unit is worth exactly P⁰k. Supplies are Xk = Σix⁰ik, and everyone starts with cash m⁰i = 50. No pair can gain from trading at this point, so nothing happens until the shock.

U⁰i, utility when trading starts. Investor i's utility at the starting position, evaluated with its post-shock preferences. It is the disagreement point of every bargain: a trade must leave both sides above where they are, and each investor's gain is measured from U⁰i.

The shock is not at P⁰. P⁰ is history: the price at which the starting portfolios were set. The shock comes after, at t = 0, and changes preferences (λ, or the default hazard h), not prices, holdings or cash. Its effect is that shocked investors' marginal values move away from P⁰. That disagreement is what makes trade worthwhile, and trading moves everyone's marginal values to the new common value P*.

04Where trading ends

A unique terminal bond allocation, but path-dependent cash

If two investors value a bond differently and the lower-valuing one holds some, a price between their valuations makes a small trade worthwhile. So trading can only stop when every holder of a bond has the same marginal value.

Pairwise stability. No pair can gain if and only if, for each bond k, every holder has the same marginal value P*k and every non-holder values it at most P*k. These are exactly the optimality conditions for maximizing Σi Vi(xi) subject to the fixed supplies. That problem is strictly concave because every bik > 0, so the terminal bond allocation x* is unique. It can be computed directly, bond by bond, by finding the P*k that clears supply.

What is not unique. Final cash, and therefore each investor's final utility, depends on who met whom and when. Total utility at x* is fixed, because total cash is fixed. A new shock changes the target.

Naming. The code calls x* "Walrasian", but the model has no central exchange. We call it the terminal allocation and P* the terminal marginal value.

06Theory · Private-information bargaining

Bargaining when the counterpart's type is private

The full-information solver gives a destination, x*, and a speed. With private types, "compute this trade" becomes "what should I offer, and what can I learn from the answer?" These results are our derivations for the deck's model; each numerical claim was checked against the engine running on this page.

Setup (estimated-utility solver). The proposer i knows its own type and all holdings. It replaces the counterpart's marginal values and curvatures by estimates âj, b̂j, proposes the formula trade Δ̂ with payment ẑ = ½(Ai(Δ̂) − Âj(Δ̂)), and j accepts only if its true gain is positive. A meeting allows up to m proposals.

What an answer reveals. The counterpart's gain is linear in its preference vector vj and in λj, with a public offset from the payment and the bond data. So each yes or no is a halfspace with a known offset. Unlike the barter model, cash fixes the units, so the type is identified in level, not just in direction. That matters, because the payment depends on the level. In (d*j, τj) the constraint is nonlinear, which is why the simulator uses particle beliefs.

Six results

Proposition 1 · Safety and destination

Information can slow the market, not move its destination

Every executed trade has both true gains positive, so bond welfare W strictly rises and the share of gain realised F(M) never falls, whatever the method. If a method converges, it ends at the same x*, because x* is the only pairwise-stable bond allocation. Private information changes how fast the market gets there and who earns the gains.

Proposition 2 · Public holdings leak types

The pre-shock state reveals every investor's type

At the calibrated state each holder's marginal value equals the public price P⁰k. With all six holdings positive, x⁰j gives six equations in the three unknowns (d*j, τj, λj), so the pre-shock type is identified. After a default-hazard shock, which is public, nothing private remains. After a risk-aversion shock, only who was hit and by how much is unknown. To make private information bite, hide holdings or pre-shock prices, or shock types privately.

Simulator: "From holdings" gives the estimated-utility solver the pre-shock type and a belief over the shock multiplier.

Proposition 3 · Who bears the error

The proposer never loses; the counterpart rejects overestimates

The proposer's true gain is always ½Ŝ > 0, because it uses its own true values and sets the payment. The counterpart's gain is ½Ŝ + (Aj − Âj), so trades fail only when the proposer overestimates the counterpart's bond gain by more than half the predicted surplus. For one interior bond with known curvature and error δ in âjk, the trade is accepted for either sign of δ exactly when |aik − âjk| > 4|δ|.

Proposition 4 · Efficiency vs. distribution

Errors cost surplus at second order and shift the split at first

The joint surplus is an exact quadratic in Δ, so an estimate that moves the trade by O(‖e‖) loses only O(‖e‖²) of surplus, by the formula above, with no local assumptions. The counterpart's share moves away from one half by Aj − Âj, which is first order. In the barter model this held only approximately.

Check: the first panel below measures both slopes on the current market.

Proposition 5 · Stalling and a price floor

Fixed errors stop the market short of x*

By Proposition 3, a pair whose valuation gap is within about four times the belief error can be blocked indefinitely. Near x* every gap is small, so without learning the estimated-utility solver stalls at F < 1 and holders' marginal values stay dispersed by an amount of order the error. A valuation gap Δp is a spread gap of about Δp/(PkTk), so for a given error the residual spread dispersion is largest at the short end.

Proposition 6 · How to judge a negotiator

Bracket it with two computable benchmarks

"If it's not converging but it's reaching somewhere, how do we say whether the LLM did a good job?" Compare it, at the same meeting and proposal budget, against two benchmarks. The true-utility solver is an upper bound on F(M). The estimated-utility solver given the same observations is an information-matched baseline, and an LLM is doing well if it beats it. Report the F(M) gap, rejections, messages and inference error. A counting argument gives a floor on learning: pinning a type to resolution δ takes about (dimension)·log₂(1/δ) yes/no answers.

Protocol and hypotheses

Keep fixed (handoff §6). Same market, seeds, shocked set and matching sequence across methods. Score every method with the true post-shock utilities, with the same W, F and Mε, counting meetings with no trade. Execute only feasible trades that strictly raise both true gains, with linear cash and unrestricted borrowing.

Vary. Information structure (what the prior knows; whether holdings and prices are public), proposals per meeting, memory, and instructions to the LLM negotiators.

Equal bilateral gains, not equal outcomes. Under the true-utility solver every meeting splits its surplus exactly 50/50, in money units after normalizing each investor's marginal utility of cash to 1. That is the claim we can make. It does not make final utilities equal: who meets whom, and when, decides how much surplus each investor gets to split. The deck's entry-timing result (section 09) is the sharpest example.

Goods-allocation fairness. EF1, EFX and PROP1 were defined for indivisible goods. Here bonds are divisible and cash is a perfectly divisible good, so exact EF and PROP are already well defined, and "up to one item" needs a lot size. Whether EFX or PROP1 says something useful about this market is still open. We report EF, EF1, PROP and PROP1 as descriptive measures, using the goods-and-chores versions because Vi is not monotone: past the ideal holding, more of a bond lowers utility.

A centralized benchmark for distribution. With transferable utility, the N-investor Nash bargaining solution from the post-shock state is x* plus transfers that give every investor the same gain, (W* − W(0))/N. The Gini of gains measures distance from that benchmark.

Envy is in dollars. Because cash is the common unit, maxj[Ui(mj, xj) − Ui(mi, xi)]+ is the transfer that would remove i's envy. We report the largest one as a percentage of the envious investor's pre-shock portfolio value. It is large because holdings differ a lot across investors with different risk aversion, and bilateral trade does not change who owns how much in total.

09Findings so far · Full information

Three results from the interim deck

These come from the team's Python simulations, not from this page. This page's engine reproduces the deck's seed-0 reference values exactly when given the same investor draws and matchings, including supplies, P*, W* − W(0) = 0.690857 and F for rounds 1–8. Its own seeds use a different random generator, so its numbers vary around the deck's.
Finding 1 · Slide 6

Prices overshoot after a localized shock

After 10% of investors triple their risk aversion, the terminal spread is only +7.5 bp above pre-shock. But round-1 executions imply spreads +93.9 bp above pre-shock, or +86.5 bp above terminal. Early trades are between very different investors, and their executed prices reflect that. Common default-risk shocks produced less reallocation and faster repricing.

Final-report goal: test which baseline properties survive when investors must infer each other's preferences.
Experiment 1

Private-information bargaining and LLM inference

Private types θi = (d*i, τi, λi), public holdings. Compare the four methods on accepted trades, welfare recovery, meetings and proposals, inference error and the distribution of gains.

On this page: all four methods in the simulator (LLMs stylised), the LLM lab for real negotiations, and the theory in section 06. Decide first whether holdings stay public (Proposition 2).

Experiment 2

Public price signals under private information

No public signal vs. recent execution prices or indicative quotes. Measure price adjustment and convergence. Public signals are not executable uniform prices.

On this page: the "public price signal" option for the estimated-utility solver. Note that round-1 prices overshoot (Finding 1), so early signals are noisy.

Experiment 3

Historical OTC transactions, data permitting

FINRA TRACE executions for bond-level adjustment after shocks. ETF–ICE index differences alone do not identify bilateral trade-price dynamics. So far only daily term structures were available.
